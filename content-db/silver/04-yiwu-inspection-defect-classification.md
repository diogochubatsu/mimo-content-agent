Yiwu inspection defect classification separates faults into critical, major and minor categories according to risk and customer impact. Understanding this classification system is essential for every importer who wants to make informed quality decisions. This guide explains each defect category with real-world examples, shows you how AQL sampling works, and provides a clear decision framework for pass/hold/fail outcomes.

## Why Defect Classification Matters Before Shipment

An inspection report should support a commercial decision, not merely list observations. Classification helps the inspector count comparable defects, apply acceptance limits and explain why a lot should pass, remain on hold or fail.

### The Business Impact of Poor Defect Classification

Without proper defect classification:
- **Over-rejection**: You may reject good products unnecessarily, wasting time and money
- **Under-rejection**: You may accept defective products, leading to customer complaints and returns
- **Dispute ambiguity**: Without clear classification standards, disputes with suppliers become subjective and difficult to resolve
- **Consistency problems**: Different inspectors may classify the same defect differently, leading to inconsistent quality across orders

## Critical Defects

A critical defect presents an unreasonable safety, regulatory or essential-function risk. Buyers commonly set an acceptance limit of zero.

### What Makes a Defect Critical?

A defect is classified as critical when it:
- Creates a safety hazard for the end user
- Violates regulatory requirements (CE, FCC, UL, CPSIA, etc.)
- Renders the product completely non-functional
- Could result in legal liability, product recalls, or customs seizure

### Examples of Critical Defects

- Exposed electrical conductors or accessible sharp edges
- A safety guard, warning or required component is missing
- Contamination, mould or a prohibited material is found
- Incorrect compliance, age or hazard labeling creates legal risk
- Lead paint on children's products
- Flammable materials in products that should be fire-resistant
- Missing mandatory safety certifications
- Battery leakage or overheating risks
- Structural failure that could cause injury

### Critical Defect Tolerance

| AQL Setting | Acceptance Limit | Rejection Point |
|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Critical = 0 | 0 defects allowed | 1 defect = reject entire lot |
| Critical = 0.065 | Very strict | Rarely used, reserved for medical/pharmaceutical |

**Industry standard**: Most importers set critical defect tolerance at zero. Even one critical defect should result in automatic rejection of the entire lot until the supplier corrects the issue.

## Major Defects

A major defect is likely to make a customer reject the product, return it or experience a significant loss of function.

### What Makes a Defect Major?

A defect is classified as major when it:
- Significantly reduces the product's functionality
- Would cause a reasonable customer to reject or return the product
- Falls below the agreed specifications by a material margin
- Affects the product's appearance in a noticeable way
- Could lead to negative reviews or customer complaints

### Examples of Major Defects

- A switch that fails
- Incorrect dimensions that prevent assembly
- Severe colour variation
- Missing accessories
- Broken retail packaging
- Cracked or chipped surfaces on visible areas
- Strong chemical odor
- Significant color mismatch between units
- Non-functional buttons or controls
- Poor adhesion (labels peeling, paint flaking)
- Structural weakness that may cause premature failure

### Major Defect Tolerance

| AQL Setting | Typical Acceptance | Typical Rejection |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Major = 1.0 | Up to 1.0% defective | Exceeds 1.0% = reject |
| Major = 2.5 | Up to 2.5% defective | Exceeds 2.5% = reject |
| Major = 4.0 | Up to 4.0% defective | Exceeds 4.0% = reject |

**Recommendation**: For most consumer products, an AQL of 2.5 for major defects is standard. For premium or branded products, consider tightening to 1.0.

## Minor Defects

A minor defect is a departure from the approved standard that does not substantially reduce normal use or saleability.

### What Makes a Defect Minor?

A defect is classified as minor when it:
- Does not affect the product's functionality
- Is not immediately noticeable to most customers
- Would not cause a reasonable customer to return the product
- Is within the range of acceptable variation for the product type
- Is a cosmetic issue that does not impact perceived quality

### Examples of Minor Defects

- A small mark in a hidden area
- A slight non-critical colour variation
- A neatness issue that most customers would tolerate
- Minor printing imperfections on non-critical surfaces
- Slight variations in texture or finish
- Small scratches on concealed surfaces
- Minor packaging creases or scuffs
- Slight color variation within acceptable range
- Tiny bubbles in plastic molding (non-structural)

### Minor Defect Tolerance

| AQL Setting | Typical Acceptance | Typical Rejection |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Minor = 2.5 | Up to 2.5% defective | Exceeds 2.5% = reject |
| Minor = 4.0 | Up to 4.0% defective | Exceeds 4.0% = reject |
| Minor = 6.5 | Up to 6.5% defective | Exceeds 6.5% = reject |

**Note**: Minor defects are the most common type found during inspection. While they do not warrant rejection on their own, a high rate of minor defects may indicate poor quality control processes at the factory.

## AQL Sampling and Defect Counting

### How AQL Works in Practice

The AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit) system uses statistical sampling to determine whether a lot passes or fails. Here is how it works:

1. **Determine lot size**: Count the total number of units in the order
2. **Select sample size**: Use the AQL table to determine how many units to inspect
3. **Inspect samples**: Check each sample unit against the product specifications
4. **Classify defects**: Assign each defect to critical, major, or minor
5. **Compare to limits**: Check if defect counts exceed the AQL acceptance limits
6. **Make decision**: Pass, hold, or reject based on the comparison

### AQL Calculation Example

**Scenario**: You ordered 5,000 silicone phone cases from Yiwu.

**Step 1**: Lot size = 5,000 units
**Step 2**: Sample size code = L (200 units)
**Step 3**: Inspector checks 200 units
**Step 4**: Results:
- Critical defects: 0 (within limit of 0)
- Major defects: 4 (limit is 10 at AQL 2.5)
- Minor defects: 8 (limit is 14 at AQL 4.0)

**Step 5**: All defect counts are within limits
**Step 6**: Decision = PASS

| 500 | 80 | 0 | 5 | 7 |
| 1,000 | 125 | 0 | 7 | 10 |
| 2,000 | 200 | 0 | 10 | 14 |

## Make Pass, Hold or Fail Decisions

1. **Pass:** Sample results remain within every agreed limit
2. **Hold:** Evidence is incomplete or defect needs technical review
3. **Fail:** One or more limits are exceeded or critical defect confirmed

### Decision Matrix

| Scenario | Defect Types | Decision | Next Step |
|----------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| All within limits | None exceeded | PASS | Proceed with shipment |
| Critical found, even 1 | Critical > 0 | FAIL | Reject entire lot |
| Major exceeds limit | Major > AQL | FAIL | Require correction |
| Minor exceeds limit | Minor > AQL | HOLD | Discuss with supplier |
| Incomplete data | Missing evidence | HOLD | Request complete report |
| Borderline results | Near limits | HOLD | Risk assessment needed |

### What to Do After a Fail Decision

1. **Document the failure**: Save the inspection report with all evidence
2. **Notify the supplier immediately**: Provide the report and request corrective action
3. **Define correction requirements**: Specify what must be fixed and by when
4. **Schedule re-inspection**: Plan another inspection after correction
5. **Consider cost impact**: Assess delay costs vs. quality risks
6. **Negotiate compensation**: If appropriate, negotiate price reduction or replacement

**Order**: 3,000 units of LED desk lamps from Yiwu supplier

**Inspection Results**:
- **Critical**: 1 unit had exposed wiring near the power connector

## FAQ

### What is AQL in quality inspection?
AQL (Acceptable Quality Limit) is a statistical sampling standard used to determine the maximum number of defects allowed in a product lot. It is defined in ISO 2859-1 (ANSI/ASQ Z1.4) and specifies sample sizes and acceptance criteria based on lot size and defect classification.

### Can a product with minor defects still pass inspection?
Yes, if the number of minor defects falls within the agreed AQL limit. Minor defects are expected in most manufacturing processes and do not automatically result in rejection. However, consistently high minor defect rates may indicate quality control issues.

### What happens if I find a critical defect?
A critical defect should result in automatic rejection of the entire lot. Even one critical defect means the product poses a safety or regulatory risk. The supplier must correct the issue before shipment can proceed.

### How do I set my AQL limits?
AQL limits should be agreed upon with your supplier before production begins. Standard limits are: Critical = 0, Major = 2.5, Minor = 4.0. For premium products, you may tighten these limits. For low-cost commodities, you may relax them slightly.

### Should I inspect 100% of products or use AQL sampling?
For most orders, AQL sampling is sufficient and cost-effective. 100% inspection is recommended only for critical products (medical devices, children's products, safety equipment) where zero defects are required.

### Who determines defect classification?
The buyer determines the defect classification criteria before inspection begins. These criteria should be documented in the purchase agreement and communicated to the inspector. The inspector applies the agreed criteria during inspection.

### What if the supplier disagrees with my defect classification?
If the supplier disputes your classification, refer to the agreed-upon product specifications and AQL limits documented in your purchase contract. If no agreement exists, consider using an independent third-party inspection company.

### How often should I inspect orders?
Inspect every order until you have established a consistent quality track record with the supplier. After 5–10 consecutive passing inspections, you may consider reducing inspection frequency, but never eliminate it entirely.

### Can I use my sourcing agent for inspection?
Yes, many sourcing agents offer inspection services. However, for high-value orders or critical products, consider using an independent third-party inspection company to ensure objectivity.

### What is the difference between AQL 1.0 and AQL 2.5?
AQL 1.0 is stricter than AQL 2.5. At AQL 1.0, fewer defects are allowed before the lot is rejected. Use AQL 1.0 for premium products or when quality is critical. Use AQL 2.5 for standard consumer products.

### Should I include defect photos in my supplier agreement?
Yes. Including example photos of critical, major, and minor defects in your supplier agreement eliminates ambiguity and reduces disputes during inspection. Visual references are much clearer than text descriptions.
