## Executive Summary

Sea freight remains the backbone of international trade, accounting for approximately 80% of global merchandise by volume. For importers sourcing from China, ocean shipping offers the most cost-effective solution for high-volume shipments, with rates significantly lower than air freight or express courier services.

In 2026, the global shipping industry continues to evolve with updated regulations, new container technologies, and improved digital tracking systems. China's major ports handle over 40% of global container throughput, making it the world's largest shipping hub. The country's strategic location and extensive port infrastructure provide importers with multiple departure points and competitive rates.

Understanding the nuances of sea freight — from container types and Incoterms to customs procedures and hidden costs — is essential for any business importing from China. A single mistake in documentation or container selection can result in delays of weeks and thousands of dollars in additional charges.

This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know about shipping from China by sea in 2026: container types, real cost breakdowns for major routes, transit times, customs requirements, Incoterms, and practical tips from industry experts.

## Section 1: Types of Sea Freight

### Subsection 1.1: Full Container Load (FCL)

Full Container Load shipping means you rent an entire container exclusively for your cargo. This is the most common method for medium to large shipments and offers the best per-unit shipping cost.

**Key Characteristics:**
- You pay for the entire container, regardless of how much cargo fills it
- Faster transit times (no consolidation needed)
- Lower risk of damage (no handling during transit)
- Better security (sealed at origin, opened only at destination)

**Best For:**
- Shipments exceeding 15 CBM
- Orders weighing over 10,000 kg
- High-value cargo requiring security
- Time-sensitive shipments

| Qingdao → New York | $2,300-3,100 | $3,400-4,600 | $3,600-4,900 |

### Subsection 1.2: Less than Container Load (LCL)

LCL shipping combines your cargo with other shippers' goods in a shared container. You pay only for the space your cargo occupies, making it ideal for smaller shipments.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Pay per CBM (cubic meter) or per ton, whichever is greater
- Minimum shipment: typically 1 CBM
- Longer transit times (consolidation and deconsolidation required)
- More handling increases damage risk

**Best For:**
- Shipments under 15 CBM
- Test orders and samples
- Small to medium e-commerce businesses
- Budget-conscious importers

### Subsection 1.3: Flat Rack Containers

Flat rack containers have no side walls or roof, making them ideal for oversized or irregularly shaped cargo that cannot fit in standard containers.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Used for machinery, vehicles, boats, construction materials
- Maximum width: 2.44 meters (without special permits)
- Maximum height: varies (usually 2.15-2.35m on deck)
- Requires special loading equipment

**Cost Premium:** 15-25% above standard FCL rates

### Subsection 1.4: Open Top Containers

Open top containers allow loading from the top, perfect for cargo that exceeds standard container height or requires crane loading.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Removable tarpaulin roof
- Internal height: typically 2.65-2.90m
- Ideal for tall machinery, generators, large equipment
- Requires weather protection during transit

**Cost Premium:** 10-20% above standard FCL rates

### Subsection 1.5: Reefer Containers (Refrigerated)

Reefer containers maintain controlled temperatures for perishable goods, pharmaceuticals, and temperature-sensitive products.

**Temperature Range:** -30°C to +30°C
**Best For:** Food products, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, cosmetics
**Cost Premium:** 30-50% above standard FCL rates

### Subsection 2.2: How to Choose Container Size

**Use 20ft when:**
- Shipment volume is 15-28 CBM
- Weight exceeds 15,000 kg
- Products are heavy (machinery, metal goods)
- Budget is limited

**Use 40ft when:**
- Shipment volume is 28-56 CBM
- Products are bulky but lighter (furniture, textiles)
- Multiple SKUs in one shipment
- Need maximum flexibility

**Use 40ft High Cube when:**
- Shipment volume exceeds 56 CBM
- Products are light but voluminous (toys, pillows)
- Need extra height for stacking
- Cost per CBM is the priority

### Subsection 2.3: Container Loading Tips

1. **Maximize space utilization** - Fill every gap; use dunnage bags for stability
2. **Weight distribution** - Place heaviest items at the bottom, centered
3. **Stacking rules** - Never exceed container weight limit; use proper stacking patterns
4. **Moisture protection** - Use desiccant bags, especially for long voyages
5. **Documentation** - Photograph loading process for insurance claims

### Subsection 3.3: Surcharges and Fees to Watch

**Peak Season Surcharge (PSS):**
- Period: October - December (pre-Christmas rush)
- Amount: $200-600 per container
- Impact: Significant during high-demand periods

**Emergency Bunker Surcharge (EBS):**
- Triggered by sudden fuel price spikes
- Amount: $100-300 per container
- Duration: Variable (typically 1-3 months)

**Congestion Surcharge:**
- Applied at congested ports
- Amount: $100-400 per container
- Common at: Los Angeles, Long Beach, Rotterdam

**War Risk Surcharge:**
- Applied to routes through conflict zones
- Amount: $50-200 per container
- May apply to certain Middle East routes

## Section 5: Customs and Documentation

### Subsection 5.1: Required Documents

**Essential Documents:**

1. **Commercial Invoice**
   - Must match purchase order exactly
   - Include: product description, quantity, value, Incoterms
   - Currency: USD or destination country currency

2. **Packing List**
   - Detailed breakdown of each package
   - Include: dimensions, weight, content description
   - Match commercial invoice quantities

3. **Bill of Lading (B/L)**
   - Original B/L required for FCL
   - Telex release for most modern shipments
   - Proof of contract of carriage

4. **Certificate of Origin**
   - May be required for preferential tariff rates
   - China issues Certificate of Origin via CCPIT
   - Some countries require specific forms

5. **Customs Declaration**
   - Import declaration form (destination country)
   - HS code classification (critical for duty rates)
   - Country of origin declaration

**Product-Specific Documents:**

| Product Type | Required Document | Issuing Authority |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Electronics | FCC/CE Certificate | Manufacturer |
| Food Products | FDA Registration | FDA |
| Cosmetics | FDA Registration + Ingredient List | FDA |
| Children's Products | CPSIA Certificate | CPSC |
| Medical Devices | FDA 510(k) Clearance | FDA |
| Textiles | Fiber Content Label | Manufacturer |

### Subsection 5.2: HS Code Classification

HS (Harmonized System) codes determine duty rates and import requirements. Incorrect classification is the most common cause of customs delays and penalties.

**How to Find Your HS Code:**
1. Use the destination country's customs website
2. Consult with a customs broker
3. Request HS code from your supplier
4. Use online HS code lookup tools

**Common HS Code Ranges:**

### Subsection 5.3: Customs Clearance Process

**Step-by-Step:**

1. **Arrival Notification** (1-2 days before arrival)
   - Carrier sends arrival notice
   - Begin customs documentation preparation

2. **Document Submission** (day of arrival)
   - Submit all required documents to customs broker
   - Pay import duties and taxes

3. **Customs Review** (1-3 days)
   - Automated system checks
   - May require manual inspection

4. **Duty Assessment** (same day)
   - Calculate duties based on HS code and value
   - VAT/GST added (varies by country)

5. **Release** (same day after payment)
   - Container released to consignee
   - Schedule pickup or delivery

## Section 7: Incoterms Explained

### Subsection 7.1: FOB (Free on Board)

**What it means:** Seller delivers goods on board the vessel at the named port of shipment. Buyer assumes all costs and risks from that point.

**Buyer pays:**
- Ocean freight
- Insurance
- Import duties
- Customs clearance
- Final delivery

**Best for:** Most common Incoterm for sea freight. Gives buyer control over shipping.

### Subsection 7.2: CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight)

**What it means:** Seller pays for cost, insurance, and freight to the named destination port. Risk transfers when goods are loaded on board the vessel.

**Seller pays:**
- Product cost
- Ocean freight
- Insurance (minimum coverage)

**Buyer pays:**
- Import duties
- Customs clearance
- Final delivery

**Best for:** When you want a single price including shipping.

### Subsection 7.3: EXW (Ex Works)

**What it means:** Seller makes goods available at their premises. Buyer bears all costs from that point.

**Buyer pays:**
- Pickup from factory
- All transport to port
- Export clearance
- Ocean freight
- Insurance
- Import duties
- Customs clearance
- Final delivery

**Best for:** Experienced importers with logistics setup in China.

### Subsection 7.4: DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)

**What it means:** Seller delivers goods to the named destination, cleared for import with all duties paid.

**Seller pays:**
- Everything (product to final delivery)

**Buyer pays:**
- Nothing (beyond purchase price)

**Best for:** Buyers who want a fixed, all-inclusive price.

### Subsection 7.5: Incoterms Comparison

| Incoterm | Risk Transfer | Seller Cost Responsibility | Best For |
|----------|---------------|---------------------------|----------|
| EXW | Factory | None beyond product | Experienced importers |
| FOB | On board vessel | To destination port | Most common |
| CIF | On board vessel | To destination port | Single price |
| DDP | At destination | Everything | Fixed cost buyers |

## Case Study

### Background

Pacific Home Goods Inc., a US-based home decor importer, was shipping 40ft containers of furniture, wall art, and decorative items from Guangzhou to Los Angeles. They experienced consistent delays and unexpected costs that were eating into their margins.

### Challenge

- Monthly shipment: 3 containers (40ft High Cube)
- Average transit time: 45 days (target: 35 days)
- Hidden cost overruns: $2,800/month average
- Customs delays: 2-3 times per month
- Customer complaints about late deliveries: 35%

### Root Cause Analysis

1. **Incorrect HS code classification** - Products were misclassified, causing customs holds
2. **Poor packaging** - Damaged goods requiring rework
3. **Wrong Incoterm** - Using EXW when they lacked China logistics expertise
4. **No pre-shipment inspection** - Quality issues caught only at destination

### Solution Implemented

1. **Engaged customs broker** - Corrected HS codes, saving 3-5 days per shipment
2. **Switched to FOB** - Let supplier handle inland transport to port
3. **Added pre-shipment inspection** - Third-party QC before loading
4. **Upgraded packaging** - Used moisture barriers and proper palletization
5. **Negotiated volume discount** - Committed to 3 containers/month for 10% rate reduction

### Results

1. **HS code accuracy saves time and money** - Invest in proper classification upfront

## FAQ

**Q: What is the cheapest way to ship from China by sea?**
A: Full Container Load (FCL) offers the lowest per-unit cost, typically $0.50-2.00 per kilogram. For smaller shipments under 15 CBM, Less than Container Load (LCL) costs $45-95 per CBM. Always compare all-inclusive door-to-door rates, not just ocean freight.

**Q: How long does sea freight take from China to the US?**
A: Transit times vary by route: Shanghai to Los Angeles takes 14-18 days, Shanghai to New York takes 22-28 days, and Shenzhen to Houston takes 25-35 days. Add 2-5 days for customs clearance and 1-3 days for final delivery for total door-to-door time.

**Q: What Incoterm should I use for sea freight from China?**
A: FOB (Free on Board) is the most common and recommended Incoterm for sea freight. It gives you control over ocean freight selection while letting the supplier handle inland transport to the port. Avoid EXW unless you have established logistics operations in China.

**Q: How much does it cost to ship a 20ft container from China?**
A: A 20ft container from China to the US West Coast costs $2,750-4,130 all-inclusive. This includes ocean freight, terminal handling, documentation, and customs clearance. Costs to Europe are $3,200-4,500, and to Brazil $3,800-5,200.

**Q: What documents do I need for sea freight from China?**
A: Essential documents include: Commercial Invoice, Packing List, Bill of Lading, Certificate of Origin, and Customs Declaration. Product-specific documents may be required (FCC for electronics, FDA for food/cosmetics). Your freight forwarder can help prepare most documents.

**Q: How do I choose between LCL and FCL?**
A: Choose FCL if your shipment exceeds 15 CBM or weighs over 10,000 kg — you'll get lower per-unit costs and faster transit. Choose LCL for smaller shipments under 15 CBM where you only pay for the space you use. For shipments 10-15 CBM, compare total costs including surcharges.

**Q: What are the hidden costs of sea freight from China?**
A: Hidden costs include: terminal handling charges ($200-600), customs clearance ($150-400), inland transport ($200-600), insurance (0.3% of cargo value), documentation fees ($50-100), and potential surcharges (peak season, congestion). Budget an extra 20-30% above the base ocean freight rate.

**Q: Can I track my sea freight shipment from China?**
A: Yes, most carriers and freight forwarders offer online tracking. You'll typically get: booking confirmation, vessel departure, estimated arrival, customs clearance status, and delivery confirmation. Digital freight platforms like Flexport provide real-time tracking with mobile apps.

**Q: What happens if my cargo is damaged during sea freight?**
A: File a claim with your freight forwarder and insurance provider immediately upon discovery. Document damage with photos and keep all packaging. Insurance claims must typically be filed within 9 months for sea freight. Prevention is better: invest in proper packaging and cargo insurance (0.3% of cargo value).

**Q: How do I reduce transit time for sea freight from China?**
A: Reduce transit time by: (1) choosing direct routes instead of transshipment, (2) selecting less congested ports, (3) booking early to secure space on faster vessels, (4) having all documentation ready before shipment, (5) using a customs broker with fast clearance capabilities.
